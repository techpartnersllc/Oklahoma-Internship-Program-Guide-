Introduction

About This Guide

Oklahoma's workforce landscape is evolving rapidly. As data centers, technology firms, healthcare systems, and energy companies expand across the state, the competition for skilled talent has never been more intense. Internship programs are one of the most cost-effective tools available — not just for filling short-term project needs, but for building a pipeline of trained, culture-aligned professionals who already know your systems, your team, and your mission.

This guide is for Oklahoma employers with 50–500+ employees who are ready to build — or significantly improve — a formal internship program. It covers strategy, structure, timelines, key contacts, tools, and the state-specific resources that make Oklahoma a particularly strong environment for these programs.

Part 1

The Oklahoma Case for Internships

The Talent Shortage Is Real

Oklahoma faces a persistent talent gap in IT, engineering, healthcare, and skilled trades. Colleges are producing graduates at a steady pace, but without structured employer pathways, many leave the state for opportunities elsewhere. A well-run internship program is a retention tool before the student even graduates.

State-Backed Incentives Exist

The Oklahoma Center for the Advancement of Science and Technology (OCAST) offers the STEM Intern Partnership Program, providing up to $30,000 per year for two years to companies that hire student interns on STEM-related projects. This is a meaningful cost offset that most employers never take advantage of.

OCAST STEM Intern Partnership — Up to $30,000/year for 2 years · Rolling applications
oklahoma.gov/ocast · (405) 319-8400

Tax Credit Opportunities

Under Oklahoma's Quality Jobs program and various workforce investment statutes, some internship wages may qualify for workforce tax credits. Consult your CPA or the Oklahoma Department of Commerce for current eligibility.

Part 2

Strategic Foundation — Before You Post the Job

Before you recruit a single intern, answer these five foundational questions:

1. What is the business goal of this program?

Programs succeed when they serve a clear purpose. Common goals: talent pipeline (convert interns to full-time hires), project capacity (complete scoped work lacking internal bandwidth), employer brand (create university visibility), or innovation (bring in fresh perspectives).

2. Which departments are ready to host interns?

Look for teams with a defined project for 10–16 weeks, a supervisor with 2–4 hours/week for mentorship, and work that's meaningful — not just administrative.

3. What positions will you offer?

Write real job descriptions with deliverables, dates, and compensation. Unpaid internships carry legal risk under the FLSA "primary benefit" test and are strongly discouraged for private-sector employers.

4. What will you pay?

5. Who owns this program?

Assign a single program owner — typically an HR Manager, Talent Acquisition Specialist, or a dedicated Internship Coordinator. This person is accountable for the full cycle.

Program Formats

  • Traditional Semester Internship (10–16 weeks) — Best for deep project work. Aligns with university academic calendars.
  • Summer Intensive (8–12 weeks) — Highest student availability. Best for cohort experiences. Ideal for first-time programs.
  • Micro-Internship (1–5 week sprints) — Low-commitment. Excellent for testing talent. Platforms like Parker Dewey facilitate these.
  • Co-Op Program (6–12 months) — Students alternate between school and full-time work. Best for engineering and IT. Requires formal university partnerships.

Onboarding Your Intern

Before Day 1

  • Workstation, credentials, and system access provisioned
  • Welcome email sent with parking, dress code, and schedule
  • Mentor/supervisor notified and briefed
  • Onboarding schedule built for Week 1

Day 1

  • Welcome meeting with HR and direct supervisor
  • Office/facility tour
  • Benefits overview and paperwork (W-4, I-9, direct deposit)
  • IT setup and tool orientation
  • Meet-the-team lunch or coffee

Week 1

  • Company overview and mission/values session
  • Department deep-dive meetings with key stakeholders
  • Project kickoff — review scope, deliverables, timeline
  • Set weekly 1:1 cadence with supervisor
Part 9

Managing and Evaluating

Check-In Cadence

  • Weekly: Supervisor meets with intern (30–60 min). Review progress, answer questions, adjust workload.
  • Midpoint (Week 5–6): Formal evaluation — written feedback from supervisor, self-assessment from intern. Address any performance concerns.
  • Final Week: Exit interview (intern) + supervisor debrief. Conversion decision documented.

Part 10

Conversion — Turning Interns Into Hires

NACE data: Companies with formal internship programs fill 36–40% of entry-level positions through intern conversions — at a fraction of the cost of external recruiting.
  • Communicate intentions early. If a conversion is likely, tell the intern by midpoint. Don't leave them guessing.
  • Extend offers before the program ends. The best interns have other offers. Don't lose them by waiting until after graduation.
  • Create a return intern track. For students with 1–2 years remaining, a return offer deepens loyalty and commitment.
  • Document what worked. Which roles converted best? Which universities? Which supervisors? Use data to improve the next cycle.

Part 12

Building Long-Term University Partnerships

A one-time career fair presence is not a partnership. Meaningful relationships require consistent investment year-round.

Touchpoints That Matter

  • Annual meeting with the career center director — not just the coordinator
  • Guest lectures and classroom visits by company leaders
  • Sponsoring or participating in student organization events
  • Hosting campus visits or facility tours
  • Providing mock interviews or resume review sessions
  • Scholarship sponsorships for high-performing students
The employers who win the best talent invest in the relationship year-round, not only during recruiting season.
Part 13

Compliance and Legal Considerations

Paid vs. Unpaid Internships

The U.S. Department of Labor's "primary beneficiary" test governs intern pay. For most private-sector employers, interns must be paid at least minimum wage. Oklahoma mirrors the federal rate ($7.25), but paying market rate is legally safer and strategically smarter.

Employment Paperwork

Treat interns as employees: W-4, I-9, direct deposit setup, and any required state withholding forms. Consult your HR team or employment attorney.

Confidentiality and IP

  • NDA / Confidentiality Agreement — covering company information they access
  • IP Assignment Agreement — if the intern will produce work product on company projects

Workers' Compensation & ADA

Interns are generally covered under your existing workers' comp policy — confirm with your carrier. Interns with disabilities are protected under the ADA; apply your normal accommodation process.
